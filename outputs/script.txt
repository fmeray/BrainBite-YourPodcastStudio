Have you ever cooked a meal using a new gadget, like an air fryer or a slow cooker? At first, it might seem like a cool addition that just simplifies your routine. But if you don’t change your entire recipe approach, the meal might not turn out any better than before. It’s the same with artificial intelligence, or AI. Many organizations today are excited about AI, treating it like a new kitchen tool, but they often forget to rethink their entire cooking process.

A recent study explored how companies can use AI effectively. The researchers discovered that just having AI tools isn’t enough to create value. Many organizations are still figuring out how to fit AI into their existing workflows, similar to how someone might try to fit a new gadget into an outdated kitchen setup. The results show that companies progressing the most with AI are those that really transform their processes instead of simply piling more tools on top of old methods. 

The study highlights three main stages, or “horizons,” of AI transformation that organizations go through. In the first horizon, called enablement, companies provide access to AI tools, but that’s about it. Employees may find using these tools easier for mundane tasks, like drafting emails or summarizing meetings, but the organization isn’t fundamentally changing how work is done. This is like getting a fancy blender but still sticking to the same old recipes.

In the second horizon, automation, organizations take a step further by automating existing workflows. Imagine if you started using that blender to mix everything before cooking, effectively speeding things up. However, many organizations still approach AI by optimizing old systems instead of reevaluating their entire kitchen design. This approach just automates routine tasks without real innovation.

The final stage is reinvention. At this point, businesses aren’t just making small adjustments; they’re overhauling how work is structured. It’s like transforming your entire cooking style, maybe you start sous-vide cooking, which requires fundamentally different planning and execution than an old-school roasting method. Unfortunately, only a small fraction of organizations have reached this advanced stage. Many are still at the earlier stages, where they think adopting AI tools is enough.

Another key finding of the study is regarding readiness. Employees often feel prepared to engage with AI, but organizations themselves are lagging behind. Think of it as a team where some members are trained in a new tactic, but the overall strategy hasn’t changed. This gap indicates that it’s not just about individuals using AI tools, but about how organizations can adapt their whole culture and operational structure around AI capabilities.

Most importantly, the study reveals that building trust is critical, no matter which stage a company is in. Trusting the organization means employees believe they will be supported through changes brought by AI, not just expected to adapt on their own. When companies emphasize building a culture of trust, they tend to succeed more in capturing meaningful value from AI initiatives.

So, the takeaway here is clear: AI is a powerful tool, but to get the most out of it, organizations must rethink their entire operational recipe. Just like cooking, it’s not just about having the right appliances; it’s about knowing how to use them in harmony with everything else. Being ahead in the AI game means transforming processes, fostering a culture of trust, and encouraging constant innovation, rather than simply piling on more technology.
